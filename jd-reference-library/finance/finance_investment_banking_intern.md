## Implications for Our Framework
- GPA-threshold sensitivity varies by firm and should be split into sub-tracks within the edu dimension: T. Rowe Price (asset management) sets a clear hard floor (3.5) with an earlier recruiting timeline, J.P. Morgan (IB) sets a lower floor (3.2), while Blackstone (PE), Morgan Stanley (S&T), and Amazon (corporate finance) give no explicit GPA figure.
- The skill profile for S&T diverges sharply from IB/PE/AM: Morgan Stanley explicitly states no finance-major requirement, focusing instead on numerical aptitude and composure under pressure, with no mention of modeling, valuation, or industry research anywhere in the posting — the "financial modeling portfolio" bonus item has low marginal value for S&T and should be flagged as "IB/PE/AM/research-track only."
- PE carries hidden gatekeeping beyond the JD text itself: Blackstone's posting doesn't literally require prior IB experience, but PE analyst programs overwhelmingly draw from candidates who already hold boutique/bulge-bracket IB internships — for students targeting PE/VC, a "top-tier IB/fund internship" should be treated as a near-prerequisite rather than a generic bonus item.
- Corporate finance/FP&A is the only track that names specific tool skills explicitly: Amazon calls out VLOOKUP, pivot tables, and VBA, and emphasizes "cross-functional influence" — unlike the valuation-modeling and industry-research emphasis of IB/PE/AM, the skill dimension under the FP&A sub-track should be refined to "advanced Excel + basic finance/accounting principles."
- None of the five postings make CFA/FRM a hard requirement, which validates the current cert weight (8 points) — but the scope of bonus item ② should narrow by sub-track: AM and IB implicitly reward "certification progress" more, while S&T and corporate finance make no mention of certifications at all.
- Leadership evaluation should split into "formal-title" and "resilience-under-pressure" archetypes by sub-track: Amazon and J.P. Morgan emphasize structured leadership and cross-team influence, while Morgan Stanley (S&T) barely mentions formal leadership, emphasizing teamwork and steady performance under pressure instead.
